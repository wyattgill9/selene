# Selene Design Doc — Technical Review ("Verify, Roast, Break Down" / 实事求是)

## TL;DR
- **The architecture is directionally sound and most of its individual technical claims check out, but the doc oversells three load-bearing pillars — the `io_uring` cancellation rationale, SO_REUSEPORT-as-free-distribution, and the "~4–5k lines" target — and it rests its entire thesis on line-count figures I could not independently verify.** Build it, but treat Phase 0 as a real go/no-go gate, not a formality.
- **Verified as correct:** compio's broad `io_uring` coverage (multishot, buffer rings, driver/executor split), its per-op boxing + mimalloc claim (sourced verbatim to Apache Iggy), monoio "slowing" and glommio effectively unmaintained, object_store's tokio dependency (which justifies the two-plane split), `await_holding_refcell_ref` existing, async-fn-in-traits still not dyn-compatible, and that helio's background-fiber scheduler is real (confirmed via DragonflyDB's own flags).
- **Wrong or overstated:** "dropping an in-flight `io_uring` future is a use-after-free in kernel space" conflates two different problems (buffer-UAF vs connection/resource leak); "every `io_uring` runtime leaks TCP connections with select!" is true but for the *leak* reason, not the UAF reason; SO_REUSEPORT is *not* clean free distribution (head-of-line blocking, hash skew, and connection drops on listener close during rolling restart are all documented); and `cargo miri on uring.rs` is largely incoherent because miri cannot execute `io_uring` syscalls.

## Key Findings

**1. The line-count argument is unverified — and it is the doc's entire thesis.** The central claim ("~90% of helio's ~74k lines exist because C++17 lacks async/await, cargo, and a networking ecosystem; Selene is the load-bearing ~10%") stands or falls on the per-directory line counts in Section 1. I confirmed the helio repo is real (`romange/helio`, Apache-2.0, ~685 commits as of late 2025) and that the named directories (`base/`, `cmake/`, `io/`, `tools/`, `util/`) exist. But **none** of the specific figures — util/fibers ≈14,292, base ≈26,080, util/http ≈8,041, util/tls ≈5,438, util/cloud+aws ≈5,922, io ≈2,369, tools/gdb_fibers.py = 535, ~74k total — could be confirmed or refuted from primary sources (GitHub tree listings and the API are not machine-fetchable without a clone). **Recommendation: run `cloc` and `git shortlog -sn` on a fresh clone and paste the raw output into the doc.** Right now the most important numbers in the document are asserted, not shown.

**2. Most named helio internals are real; several specific ones are unconfirmed.** Verified to exist in helio/Dragonfly source: `PickConnectionProactor`, `ListenerInterface` (with `OnMaxConnectionsReached`), `ProactorPool::DispatchBrief`/`AwaitBrief`/`AwaitFiberOnAll`, `FiberAtomicGuard`, the `fu2`/function2 task type, `CondVarAny`/`Mutex`/`BlockingCounter`, `UringProactor` with `RegisterBufferRing` + provided-buffer receive + `EnableRecvMultishot`, and the `--use_incoming_cpu` flag mapping to `SO_INCOMING_CPU` (this last one confirmed directly in `util/http/http_main.cc`). **Could not confirm** (neither in the doc's favor nor against): `migrate_traversal_state_` and its 32/32 bit-split design, `PrintAllFiberStackTraces`, `tools/gdb_fibers.py`, the `MainLoop` "270 lines / jump-attribution histogram / 500µs budget" description, `DispatchOnAll`, the `capacity_fixed<16,8>` / `static_assert(sizeof(Tasklet)==32)` detail, the six other `ListenerInterface` hooks, and the full `synchronization.h` primitive set. These are plausible and internally consistent but should be marked as "from memory / to be cited," not stated as fact.

**3. The background-fiber scheduler is real, but the exact constants are unverified.** DragonflyDB's public flags page lists, verbatim, "Background fiber budget in nanoseconds" and "Sleep probability of background fibers on reaching budget." This independently confirms the *existence* of helio's two-tier NORMAL/BACKGROUND budgeted scheduler with probabilistic sleeping — a genuinely non-trivial policy that Dragonfly depends on. However, the specific numbers (1ms NORMAL round-robin, 50µs BACKGROUND, 10% warrant, "punish the hog" 50ms-vs-10ms reset) were not found in reachable source and should be cited or softened. The doc is right that plain compio/monoio give you "FIFO with no fairness" and that this policy must be rebuilt — this is one of the harder, more honest parts of the doc.

**4. compio claims are largely accurate and well-supported.** compio is documented as "a thread-per-core Rust runtime with IOCP/io_uring/polling," inspired by monoio, shipping `fs`, `net`, `tls`, `quic`, a dispatcher, buffer pool, and a decoupled driver. Its 2026 releases add multishot IO (`ReadMulti`, `RecvMulti`, `RecvFromMulti`, `RecvMsgMulti`, `AcceptMulti`), a new low-level `compio-executor` crate, a full `io_uring` fallback path, and edition-2024 support — consistent with "actively maintained 2026" and "broadest io_uring coverage." Critically, the doc's specific claims are corroborated by Apache Iggy's February 2026 migration writeup, which chose compio for exactly these reasons: broad `io_uring` coverage, patches "merged within hours," and a codebase "where the driver is disaggregated from the executor, meaning one can build their own executor while still reusing the io_uring driver." Iggy also states verbatim that compio "boxes the I/O request… every I/O request incurs a heap allocation… it's not that big of a deal, as those allocations are very small and mimalloc is quite good at maintaining a pool for small, predictable allocations" — which is precisely the doc's Section 2.1 claim, correctly attributed.

**5. The DST claim is aspirational, not proven.** The doc says compio "enables deterministic simulation testing via driver/executor split." Iggy confirms the *structural* enabler (driver/executor decoupling) but explicitly warns: "At this moment, with async Rust, it is very difficult, if not borderline impossible, to achieve total determinism," because you cannot easily replace the executor's internal time wheel. No one has shipped DST on compio. The doc should downgrade this from a feature to a research bet, and Phase 3 should be scoped accordingly.

**6. The cancellation rationale (Rule 6.1) is good advice built on a wrong explanation.** The prohibition on `select!`-ing over `io_uring` I/O futures is *sound engineering*, but the doc's justification is muddled. Two distinct problems are being conflated: (a) **buffer use-after-free** — if you drop a future whose buffer the kernel still owns, the kernel may write into freed memory; this is the withoutboats/yoshuawuyts concern, and compio's completion-based owned-buffer model (BufResult) genuinely does address it by moving buffer ownership into the operation; and (b) **resource/connection leak (halt safety)** — the tonbo demonstration ("Async Rust is not safe with io_uring," Oct 2024) shows that using `select!` for timeouts leaks TCP connections across *all* io_uring runtimes tested, because the kernel completes the accept after the timeout branch is taken. Problem (b) is a *leak*, not a kernel-space UAF, and compio's buffer model does **not** automatically solve it. So: "every io_uring runtime leaks TCP connections when select! is used for timeouts" is **verified true**; "dropping an in-flight future is a use-after-free in kernel space" is **misleading** as stated. The rule ("timeouts must go INTO the operation") is correct and worth keeping — but the doc should fix the reasoning so engineers understand *which* failure they are preventing.

**7. SO_REUSEPORT is not the clean free lunch the doc implies — and it deletes a whole protocol.** Section 2.2 leans hard on "the kernel distributes." Documented realities: (a) NGINX/F5's "Socket Sharding in NGINX Release 1.9.1" writeup (wrk, 4 workers, 36-core AWS instance) found "reuseport increases requests per second by 2 to 3 times," but warns verbatim that "when a worker is stalled by a blocking operation, the block affects not only connections that the worker has already accepted, but also connection requests that the kernel has assigned to the worker since it became blocked" — head-of-line blocking; (b) severe skew is documented in production and in LWN's SO_REUSEPORT coverage, which notes that at Google "they have seen a factor-of-three difference between the thread accepting the most connections and the thread accepting the fewest connections" — i.e., the "3×" imbalance is a real, measured phenomenon, not just theory; (c) the classic NGINX ticket #1300 problem — closing a SO_REUSEPORT socket makes Linux drop all connections queued on it, which bites during rolling restart/config reload. Kuniyuki Iwashima's kernel patch series (PATCH v5 bpf-next, May 2021) added SO_REUSEPORT socket migration (via `SO_ATTACH_REUSEPORT_[CE]BPF`) to fix the drop-on-close case, describing the defect verbatim: "when the listener is closed, in-flight requests during the three-way handshake and child sockets in the accept queue are dropped even if other listeners on the same port could accept such connections. This situation can happen when various server management tools restart server (such as nginx) processes." That fix requires an eBPF program and a recent-enough kernel. For a database with long-lived connections and graceful-restart requirements, this is a real operational surface the doc waves away. The "connection migration would be ~30 lines" claim is optimistic given fd-passing + re-registration + in-flight-op draining on the source shard.

**8. glommio is effectively unmaintained; monoio is slowing; the "~150 lines" replacement of glommio's scheduler is naive.** Verified: glommio has an open memory-corruption report (#701, `spsc_queue` under concurrent use, Dec 10 2025) and a "Call for glommio maintaners" issue (#707), and Iggy calls it "pretty much unmaintained." monoio: Iggy says it "doesn't appear to be very actively maintained… still receives patches… but the overall pace doesn't keep up" — matching the doc's "slowing." The doc's claim that glommio's proportional-share scheduler can be rebuilt "in ~150 lines above the runtime" understates the problem: glommio runs **three** `io_uring` rings per thread (main, latency, poll) and its scheduler is *latency-aware and integrated with the submission loop*. A userspace cooperative budget guard (`budget.tick().await`) can approximate *priority/fairness* but cannot replicate latency-ring integration. Call it an approximation with a named ceiling, per the doc's own Rule 4.

**9. object_store validates the two-plane split — and its bus factor is better than the doc admits.** object_store is "built on tokio" and its aws/azure/gcp features "each bundle a reqwest-based HTTP transport." It therefore *requires a tokio reactor*, which is exactly why the doc correctly puts it on the control-plane tokio runtime. It does cover S3+GCS+Azure at a useful level and is production-grade (used by crates.io and InfluxDB IOx, governed by Apache Arrow). This is a place where the doc's Section 4.1 "maintenance transfer to ~60 crates each with bus factor ~1" pessimism is **too harsh**: object_store is an Apache project with many maintainers, not a solo effort. The bus-factor argument is fair for compio itself but wrong for object_store, rustls, tokio, and axum.

**10. quanta's TSC path has a platform gap that matters for Dragonfly.** quanta only uses the TSC on x86/x86_64 with constant/invariant TSC, and falls back to stdlib timing otherwise; docs warn "'constant' TSC doesn't account for all possible power states." **There is no TSC fast-path on aarch64.** DragonflyDB publishes its headline benchmarks on AWS Graviton — its "2024 New Year, New Number" post reports "6.43 million ops/second… on a new AWS c7gn.16xlarge single instance," powered by "ARM-based AWS Graviton3E processors" (up from "4 million ops/sec on an AWS c6gn.16xlarge instance"). So the TSC-timed `budget.tick()` degrades to a slower clock on precisely the hardware Dragonfly markets on. The scheduler still works, but the doc should state the ARM caveat explicitly (per its own Rule 4).

**11. The Rust-specific technical claims mostly hold, with two corrections.** (a) async fn in traits is still *not* dyn-compatible as of the 2026 nightly toolchains; the doc's escape hatch ("the shard is generic over `S: Service`") is the correct static-dispatch answer and needs no boxing — **verified correct** (crates like `dynosaur`/`trait-variant` exist precisely because native dyn-async is still unshipped). (b) `await_holding_refcell_ref` is a real clippy lint that catches `Ref`/`RefMut` held across `.await` — but it is a *separate* lint from `await_holding_lock` (which is for `Mutex`/`RwLock` guards). The doc's line that the mutex rule "becomes clippy's await_holding_lock" is imprecise for the RefCell case; you need both lints, and both have known false-positive/false-negative edge cases (e.g., async-block false negatives, issue #6904). (c) `#![deny(unsafe_code)]` crate-wide-except-uring.rs is realistic *because* socket2/rustix/quanta/pprof encapsulate the raw syscalls, thread pinning, TSC reads, and signal handling in safe APIs — the carve-out for uring.rs is the right call. (d) `cargo miri on uring.rs` is **largely incoherent**: miri does not support the `io_uring` syscall surface, so it cannot execute the actual ring code — it can only check non-syscall unsafe logic. This CI step should be reworded or dropped. (e) "per-connection memory drops from kilobytes of stack to hundreds of bytes of state machine" is *directionally* correct (stackless coroutines vs stackful fibers is a genuine win) but optimistic in magnitude: async state machines suffer the well-known large-future problem, and connection buffers (BytesMut) live outside the state machine as separate allocations.

**12. pprof-based stall detection is viable but has documented signal-safety limits.** The watchdog design (sample a per-shard heartbeat, signal the thread, capture a backtrace) is feasible with tikv's pprof-rs, but that crate itself warns that libgcc's unwinder "is not signal safe," recommends a blocklist (`libc`, `libgcc`, `pthread`, `vdso`), uses `try_lock` in the handler to avoid deadlock, and cannot use `malloc` in the handler. The 80-line estimate is plausible for a happy path; robust signal-safe capture is fiddlier than the doc implies.

## Details

**On the "9× / 45k→4k" line-reduction estimate.** This is presented as near-arithmetic but is really a forecast, and rewrite forecasts are systematically optimistic (second-system effect, and the tendency to count the happy path while discounting edge-case categories). Even granting that Rust's ecosystem legitimately deletes vendored TLS/HTTP/cloud/JSON code, the ~4–5k target omits or under-budgets several categories that helio's 45k almost certainly contains: per-OS error-path handling, backpressure/flow control, connection-limit and slowloris/timeout defense, memory accounting and graceful degradation under OOM (which Dragonfly cares about intensely — its own `maxmemory`/RSS-ratio flag rejects new non-admin connections under memory pressure), TLS session resumption/tickets, and platform quirks. I did not find a directly comparable published C++→Rust systems rewrite with before/after line counts in this pass, so treat the 9× as an *aspiration*, not a benchmark. A more defensible framing: "we expect a large reduction; the target is a hypothesis Phase 0–2 will test."

**On the two-runtime architecture.** Running a small tokio `multi_thread` runtime beside `!Send` compio shards is workable but has sharp edges the doc should name: (a) any control-plane crate that assumes an ambient tokio reactor (object_store, hickory-resolver) must be called from within a tokio context — a stray call on a shard thread panics; (b) tokio's blocking pool can grow large by default — its `runtime::Builder` docs state the `max_blocking_threads` "default value is 512" and warn "the queue does not apply any backpressure, it could potentially grow unbounded" (Iggy flagged this thread-pool behavior as a reason for leaving tokio), which competes with pinned shards for CPU; (c) CPU accounting: if the shards pin to every core, the tokio threads have nowhere to run without oversubscribing a shard core — you must reserve a core or accept jitter on one shard. None of these is fatal; all belong in the risks table with mitigations.

**On AsioStreamAdapter.** `util/asio_stream_adapter.h` is confirmed to exist in helio (included by `echo_server.cc`). The doc's causal claim — that helio needed it *because* admin lives on the data plane — is plausible but not the whole story: Boost.Beast's HTTP machinery is written against the Asio stream concept regardless of where it runs, so the adapter would be needed to use Beast on helio's fiber sockets even if admin were isolated. State this as "a contributing reason," not "the reason."

**On the thread-per-core premise and open question #4.** The "3× overprovisioning penalty from queueing theory" is the right *instinct* but I could not locate the specific source of the "3×" figure as a *queueing* result; note, however, that the LWN datapoint above shows a measured 3× SO_REUSEPORT *distribution* imbalance at Google, which may be what the doc is half-remembering. The relevant literature is the M/M/N-vs-N×M/M/1 tail-latency argument (e.g., the COREC single-queue paper argues a shared queue beats per-core queues under imbalance) versus the shared-nothing cache-locality argument (Seastar/ScyllaDB, and withoutboats' thread-per-core essay citing Enberg). The doc's open question — *does the penalty apply to Dragonfly's access pattern?* — is exactly the right question, and the honest answer is that a KV store with hot keys **does** experience shard skew, and SO_REUSEPORT adds a *second* independent skew (connection distribution) on top of key skew. That compounding deserves a line in the risks table.

**On DragonflyDB's own stance.** Dragonfly is written in C++ by Roman Gershman (ex-Google, ex-AWS ElastiCache principal) and helio is *actively developed* (commits through late 2025). I found no public statement from the Dragonfly maintainers endorsing or planning a Rust rewrite. Selene is therefore a third-party/greenfield bet against the direction of an actively maintained upstream — the single biggest unstated strategic risk. If the goal is for Dragonfly to adopt Selene, that path is all-or-nothing (helio is the substrate, not a plugin) and requires maintainer buy-in the doc does not evidence.

## Recommendations
1. **Before writing code, replace every asserted number with measured output.** Paste `cloc util/fibers base util/http util/tls util/cloud util/aws io` and `git shortlog -sn --all` into Sections 1 and 4.1. If the counts come back materially different, the "90/10" thesis needs restating. This is the cheapest, highest-value edit.
2. **Rewrite Rule 6.1's justification.** Keep the rule ("timeouts go into the operation, never `select!` over an in-flight io_uring future"), but state the two real failure modes separately: buffer-UAF (which compio's owned-buffer model prevents) and connection/halt leaks (which it does *not* — you must use compio's cancellation path). Add a test that asserts no fd leak under timeout churn, mirroring tonbo's repro.
3. **Treat SO_REUSEPORT as a design risk, not a deletion.** Add explicit handling for: rolling-restart connection drops (require a kernel with reuseport-migration eBPF support, or drain gracefully), skew monitoring (per-shard connection-count gauge — expect up to ~3× imbalance), and head-of-line blocking (the watchdog helps). Keep the explicit-migration path as a fallback rather than assuming SO_INCOMING_CPU/CBPF covers everything.
4. **Downgrade DST from feature to research bet** (Phase 3), and fix the CI plan: drop or reword "cargo miri on uring.rs" since miri can't run io_uring syscalls — use Loom for the non-syscall concurrency logic and a real-kernel integration suite for the ring code.
5. **Soften Section 4.1's bus-factor pessimism** for the Apache/Tokio-governed crates (object_store, rustls, tokio, axum) while keeping it honest for compio, quanta, and flume — flume's own README states it "is in casual maintenance mode… will continue to receive critical security and bug fixes, but heavy feature development has stopped," which is fine for a cold-path channel but worth a note.
6. **Add the missing sections:** memory accounting/OOM behavior, backpressure, per-layer timeouts, connection limits/slowloris, TLS session resumption, graceful restart, the actual Dragonfly integration surface (what Dragonfly calls into helio for), an incremental-adoption/FFI path, and NUMA/hugepages. Add an ARM/quanta caveat to Section 3.2.
7. **Make Phase 0 measure what actually breaks the thesis.** See below.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that change the plan:** if `cloc` shows hand-written (non-vendored, non-test) C++ is materially above ~45k or the "boilerplate" fraction is well under 90%, the effort case weakens. If Phase 0 shows compio >10% behind helio on p99.99 *or* shows fd leaks under timeout churn that can't be closed cheaply, stop. If control-plane tokio + pinned shards can't coexist without >1 core of jitter, reconsider putting object_store in-process.

**What Phase 0 currently measures vs. what it should add:** the doc measures throughput, p99, p99.99, and bytes/connection against helio's echo_server, and verifies buffer rings/multishot/direct-FDs/SO_REUSEPORT accept — good. It should *also* measure: (a) **fd/connection leak rate under timeout+cancellation churn** (the tonbo failure mode); (b) **connection-distribution skew across shards under uneven client load** (the SO_REUSEPORT risk — measure the max/min shard ratio, not just aggregate throughput); (c) **behavior during graceful restart** (drop-on-close); (d) **the two-runtime CPU-contention cost** on a fully-core-packed box; (e) **the scheduler's background-vs-foreground fairness** under mixed load, since that policy is the thing Dragonfly actually depends on and plain compio lacks; and (f) **tail latency on ARM/Graviton**, where the TSC path is unavailable.

## Caveats
- The per-directory line counts, several specific helio internals (`migrate_traversal_state_`, `MainLoop` structure, `gdb_fibers.py`, the exact scheduler constants, the two-S3-stack / BIO-pair-TLS / html-status-page specifics), and the exact "517/700" commit split could **not** be verified from primary sources in this pass because GitHub's tree/API/contributor endpoints are not machine-fetchable without a clone; they are marked *unverified*, not *false*. The doc's author can settle all of them in five minutes with a local clone.
- rustls's audit status and its performance-vs-OpenSSL for a database workload were **not** independently verified in this session (search budget exhausted before that query); the doc's reliance on rustls is reasonable on reputation but should be backed with the specific audit citation and a workload benchmark.
- The two-runtime pitfalls and the "3×" queueing figure are presented as analysis/inference from adjacent primary sources, not from a single authoritative queueing-theory citation; treat them as well-grounded engineering judgment rather than settled fact.
- Where the doc is right, it is right for good reasons: the plane split, the choice of compio over monoio/glommio, the RefCell/`!Send`/no-Arc shard model, the "no abstraction with one implementation" discipline, and the honest framing of the scheduler policy and the maintenance-transfer tradeoff are all sound. This is a strong doc that needs its three load-bearing claims hardened, not a weak one.
